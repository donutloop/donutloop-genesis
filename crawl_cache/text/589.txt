Parallel research is when multiple investigators work alongside one another, contributing discrete pieces to a larger topic. Collaborative research is different. It is integrative by design. It is structured around shared questions, shared methods, shared accountability, and—most importantly—shared intellectual ownership. It produces knowledge that no single investigator or siloed team could produce alone. I want to begin by highlighting the leadership of our humanities community. The National Endowment for the Humanities Collaborative Research program explicitly supports teams producing joint interpretive work, with multi-year awards designed to advance humanistic knowledge through shared inquiry. Likewise, the American Council of Learned Societies has elevated collaborative models through its fellowship and grant programs, signaling that team-based scholarship in the humanities is not peripheral—it is central to the future of the field. This emphasis is not limited to NEH. The National Science Foundation continues to prioritize convergent, cross-directorate teams (e.g., wildfire science, Arctic systems, security and trust). The National Institutes of Health is expanding networked and multi-site mechanisms. The Department of Energy ’s Genesis Mission emphasizes integrated AI + science + deployment partnerships across labs, universities, and industry. Major foundations are favoring interdisciplinary, multi-investigator, and cross-sector models tied to real-world impact. This is not a short-term shift. It reflects how complex problems are now defined: climate resilience, wildfire mitigation, AI governance, misinformation, health equity, food security, infrastructure modernization. These are systems-level challenges. They demand teams. For Utah, this presents both opportunity and responsibility. The institutions that will thrive in this environment are those that treat collaboration as infrastructure rather than aspiration. That means cultivating durable, trust-based, interdisciplinary teams well before a solicitation drops. It means designing projects around integrative questions from the outset. It means aligning seed funding, research development support, shared instrumentation, and cross-college connections around team science and team humanities. The funding windows ahead (across NEH competitions, NSF clusters, NIH network mechanisms, DOE initiatives, and foundation cycles) strongly favor teams that are already aligned and prepared. If you are thinking about a 2026 submission—now is the time to: Identify complementary partners across colleges. Articulate a shared research question that cannot be answered alone. Build in dissemination and impact pathways early. Engage Research Development to shape the team narrative. The competitive advantage will not belong to the lone investigator. It will belong to institutions that can organize collaboration at scale. We intend to be one of them. If you are building a collaborative team—or want to—reach out. Let’s design the next wave of integrative research together. — Jakob D.
